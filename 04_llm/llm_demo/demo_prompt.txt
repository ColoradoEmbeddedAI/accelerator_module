A smart doorbell spends almost all of its life asleep. A tiny motion sensor draws a few microamps and watches the porch. When something moves, the sensor wakes a microcontroller, which turns on the camera, grabs a few frames, and runs a small neural network that asks one question: is this a person? Most of the time the answer is no. A cat walks by, a branch moves in the wind, a car's headlights sweep across the door. The microcontroller writes nothing, turns the camera off, and goes back to sleep within a fraction of a second. Only when the network is confident that a person is standing at the door does the device power up its radio, connect to the home network, and send a notification and a short clip to the owner's phone. This design is not an accident. The radio is by far the most expensive part of the system in energy: a few seconds of Wi-Fi can cost more than thousands of inferences on the microcontroller. So the engineers moved the decision as close to the sensor as they could, and spent their effort making the network small enough to fit in the chip's memory and fast enough to finish before the person rings the bell. The network was trained on many thousands of labeled images, then quantized to eight-bit integers, pruned, and compiled for the microcontroller's instruction set. Its weights live in flash memory and its activations in a small on-chip SRAM, and the developers checked the linker map to make sure the peak activation memory fit with room to spare. They measured the time per inference with a cycle counter and the energy per inference with a current probe, and they tuned the wake-up threshold so that false alarms stayed rare without missing real visitors. The battery now lasts most of a year. The same pattern appears in hearing aids, wearable health monitors, factory vibration sensors, and voice-activated light switches: sense locally, decide locally, and spend energy on communication only when the answer matters.
